# AI Protein Design

## Definition & Why It Matters

**AI protein design** is the use of machine learning models — generative diffusion models, sequence predictors, and structure predictors — to invent brand-new proteins that fold into a target shape and bind tightly to a chosen molecule, rather than screening or evolving existing proteins in the lab.

- Think of it like **inverse rendering**: instead of predicting a protein's 3D shape from its sequence (what AlphaFold does), these models start from a *desired shape* — e.g. "something that clamps onto this exact patch of this target protein's surface" — and generate a sequence that will fold into it. It's generation conditioned on a geometric constraint, similar to how a generative image model can be conditioned on a mask or pose.
- It matters because traditional protein engineering (directed evolution, rational design from crystal structures) can take **months to years per target**. AI-generated "minibinders" — small, stable proteins engineered to bind a target — can go from computer to a lab hit-rate estimate in **days**, which changes the economics of early-stage drug discovery, diagnostics, and biosensors.
- The August 2026 Anthropic research announcement ["Claude accelerates protein design"](https://www.anthropic.com/research/Claude-accelerates-protein-design) is a demonstration of an AI *agent* (Claude) autonomously orchestrating these design/folding models end-to-end — not a new design model itself, but evidence that a general-purpose LLM can drive the existing toolchain well enough to match or beat published hit rates.

## Key Techniques & Approaches

- **RFdiffusion** — a denoising diffusion model (from the Baker Lab) for generating novel protein *backbones*. Given a target structure (PDB file) and a set of "hotspot" residues on its surface, it generates candidate backbone shapes constrained to contact that spot — conceptually like a diffusion image model denoising toward a shape that satisfies a spatial mask.
- **ProteinMPNN** — a graph neural network that, given a backbone shape, predicts the amino-acid sequence most likely to actually fold into it, one residue at a time conditioned on structural neighbors — the "decoder" step after RFdiffusion's "generator" step.
- **Folding/validation models** (e.g. AlphaFold-family tools) — used afterward to check that the generated sequence, when folded computationally, actually reproduces the intended structure before anything is sent to a wet lab — analogous to a discriminator/verifier pass after a generator, filtering out designs before they cost real synthesis money.
- **Agentic orchestration** (what the Anthropic piece specifically demonstrates) — rather than a human running RFdiffusion → ProteinMPNN → folding-check → pick-best manually, an LLM (Claude, tested as Mythos Preview, Opus 4.8, and Opus 5) was given an extensive protein-design prompt, GPU access to run these specialized models, internet access, and connectors (Google Drive, Slack, Gmail, BioRxiv), and ran the full design-optimize-select loop itself with minimal human involvement beyond initial access approvals.
- **Two execution modes** were tested: a **multi-target mode** (48-hour wall time, ~12,500 total GPU-hours, working across many targets at once) and a **single-target mode** (24 hours and ~2,500 GPU-hours per target, going deep on one target at a time) — single-target mode produced a notably higher hit rate (35.1% vs. 22.6–26.7%), suggesting focused compute beats spread-out compute for this task, similar to how allocating more inference-time compute to one hard problem often beats splitting it across many easier ones.

## Landscape

- **Baker Lab (University of Washington)** — originators of RFdiffusion and ProteinMPNN, the de facto standard open-source toolchain most of the field (including this Anthropic work) builds on.
- **Adaptyv Bio** — runs the wet-lab side of this landscape as an open, Kaggle-style benchmarking service: submit a design, they express and test it, you get real binding-affinity data back. Ran a well-known 2024 EGFR binder competition (1,800+ designs submitted, winning affinity 8.2x stronger than the reference antibody Cetuximab) and more recently built **BenchBB**, a standardized 7-target benchmark (PD-L1, EGFR, IL7Ra, BHRF1, SpCas9, BBF-14, MBP) specifically so different design methods can be compared on a level playing field. Adaptyv independently produced and tested Claude's designs in this Anthropic study, alongside **Twist Bioscience** (a DNA/protein synthesis provider).
- **Cradle** — a commercial AI protein-design platform; won Adaptyv's 2024 EGFR competition.
- **Anthropic** — not a biotech company, but this research positions Claude as an *agent layer* on top of the existing open-source design/folding stack rather than a competing model — its contribution is autonomous orchestration and iteration, not a new architecture.
- Adaptyv's ongoing/expanding competitions (e.g. a Nipah virus neutralizer design challenge) suggest this benchmark-driven, crowdsourced validation loop is becoming the field's standard way to compare AI design methods against real lab data rather than simulation alone.

## Open Problems & Bottlenecks

- **Structurally hard targets remain hard.** In the Anthropic study, Claude succeeded on 14 of 15 benchmark targets but produced zero confirmed binders against **MBP** (a large, flexible bacterial protein with a smooth, featureless surface — nothing for a binder to "grab") and only partial success against **BBF-14** (an artificial, de novo protein with no natural analog). Smooth/flexible/synthetic surfaces remain a known weak point across the whole field, not just this study.
- **Inconsistent results across models on the same target** — researchers reported "uncertainty" about why Opus 4.8 succeeded on the TNFα target while Mythos Preview failed, suggesting the orchestration/agentic layer itself introduces variance that isn't yet well understood, separate from the underlying design models' capabilities.
- **Wet-lab validation is still the bottleneck**, not compute — even with GPU-heavy campaigns (thousands of GPU-hours), every promising design still has to be physically synthesized and tested (via partners like Adaptyv/Twist) before it's a real result, and the Anthropic team itself flagged that more extensive characterization is still needed to confirm the reported hit rates and affinities.
- **Dual-use safety gating** — Anthropic explicitly restricts these life-sciences/protein-design capabilities from general access in its most capable public model (Claude Fable 5) due to dual-use biosecurity risk, meaning the most capable version of this capability is not something an outside researcher can currently just go use.
- **Analytical chemistry as a parallel, less-discussed thread** — the same Anthropic study also had Claude interpret raw NMR and LC-MS instrument files (no vendor software) with results close to lab-reported values (hydrogen counts within 0.08 units; purity 96.4% vs. 96.33% lab-measured, in ~20 minutes per analysis) — a much less mature but potentially high-leverage adjacent application: AI doing analytical/QC chemistry interpretation, not just generative design.

## Personal Takeaways

- This is a clean example of the "agent orchestrating existing tools beats a bespoke pipeline" pattern — Claude isn't a better protein design model than RFdiffusion/ProteinMPNN, it's a better *operator* of them, hitting 22–35% hit rates against a stated industry standard of 10–15%. Worth watching whether this generalizes: is the gain from better target/hotspot selection, better iteration loops, or something else?
- The MBP/BBF-14 failure cases are more interesting than the successes for understanding the actual limits of the technology — smooth, featureless, or fully synthetic surfaces seem to be where both the design models and the orchestrating agent break down.
- Adaptyv Bio's BenchBB is worth tracking as a recurring, standardized way to compare any new AI protein design approach (agentic or not) against a common set of real wet-lab-tested targets, rather than relying on self-reported in-silico numbers.
- Open question to revisit: Anthropic says data/prompts/models are public via Hugging Face and that an access program for scientists is planned — worth checking back on whether that program is live and what it takes to get in, since right now this capability is gated out of general Claude access.

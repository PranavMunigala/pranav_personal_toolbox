# Fairness-Aware Skin Cancer Detection AI (FAIR-SCAN)

## Definition & Why It Matters

AI models that scan photos of skin lesions to flag possible skin cancer are being built as a triage tool — something that could one day help a primary-care doctor or telehealth app decide "does this mole need to see a dermatologist now?" The problem: these models have consistently performed worse on darker skin (Fitzpatrick Skin Types V-VI) than on lighter skin, because the photo datasets used to train them are overwhelmingly light-skinned — the AI simply never got enough dark-skin examples to practice on.

- **The stakes are inverted from what you would guess**: melanoma is *less* common in darker-skinned patients, but survival rates are *lower* — largely due to later diagnosis, less access to care, and (per this line of research) AI tools that are worse at catching it in the first place.
- **This paper is a 2026 *Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association* (JAMIA) study**, "Fairness aware subset selection for advancing equity in skin cancer detection," by Yehuda Perry, Abdulaziz A. Almuzaini, Adewole S. Adamson, Bahar Dasgeb, David J. Foran, and Vivek K. Singh (several from Rutgers). It introduces **FAIR-SCAN** (Fairness and Accuracy through Ranking-Based Subset Selection for Skin Cancer Detection).
- **The core idea, in plain terms**: most researchers try to fix this kind of bias by tweaking the AI's math — the model itself. This team instead asked *what if the problem is actually which photos we're feeding it, not the model?* So instead of changing the AI, they changed the training data — they went looking for which photos were actually helping the model learn fairly, and which ones were secretly making it worse, then trained only on the best subset. Think of it like feature selection in a regression problem, except instead of picking which columns of data to use, you are picking which *rows* (images) actually deserve to be in the training set.

## Key Techniques & Approaches

**The general problem area — data-centric AI for fairness**: most bias-fixing research in medical AI tweaks the model architecture or loss function (algorithm-centric). FAIR-SCAN instead treats data curation itself as the lever — a deliberate garbage-in-garbage-out fix, not just an afterthought.

- **Marginal Contribution Score (MCS) — the heart of the method**: figuring out which photos were "good" or "bad" for the model works like a group project where everyone contributes differently — some teammates make the project way better, some barely help, and some actually drag it down. There's a classic method for fairly scoring how much each person contributed to a group's success — the **Shapley value**, from game theory. FAIR-SCAN applies that same idea, but with *photos* instead of people:
  1. Train a small starter model (InceptionV3, a well-known image-classification CNN) on just 10% of the photos.
  2. Test each remaining photo one at a time: if I add this one photo to training, does accuracy go up? Does the gap between light-skin and dark-skin performance shrink or grow?
  3. Give each photo a score combining both effects — better accuracy is good, a worse fairness gap is a penalty.
  - The composite score is: Marginal Composite Score = Marginal Accuracy minus lambda times Marginal Bias Cost, with lambda (they used 1.0) controlling the weighting. If you have done regularized regression, this is structurally the same idea as an accuracy term plus a penalty term.
- **Rank and select top K%**: every photo gets an MCS, photos are sorted best-to-worst, and only the top-scoring slice (they tested 25%/50%/75%/100%) is used to fine-tune the actual deployed model, built on **SAFE** (Skin Cancer Detection with Adaptive Fairness-aware Ensemble — a prior algorithm-centric fairness method from the same Rutgers group), which itself sits on **DeepDerm**, which sits on **InceptionV3**.
- **The surprising result**: training on only the **top-ranked 50% of photos beat using all of them (100%)** — both more accurate (92.2% vs. 89.9%) and more fair (the true-positive-rate gap between light and dark skin dropped from 9.33 points to 5.03 points). In other words, throwing more data at the problem didn't help — some photos were actively teaching the model bad habits, and cutting them out fixed things. The paper's headline finding: **scaling up your dataset does not automatically make an AI model more equitable**; it matters *which* data, not how much.
- **Random subset selection**, tested as a baseline, was far worse than the ranked approach — dropping to roughly 80% accuracy at 50% of the data with a smaller fairness edge, showing the gains come from *which* images are chosen, not just training on less data.
- Evaluation used two real dermatology datasets — **DDI** (Diverse Dermatology Images, from Stanford, 656 pathology-confirmed images spanning all Fitzpatrick types) and **Fitzpatrick17k** (16,577 images) — and standard fairness metrics: delta-Accuracy, delta-TPR (true positive rate gap — how much more often cancer is *missed* in one group vs. another), and delta-FPR (false positive rate gap — how much more often a benign spot is misclassified as cancer).

## Landscape

- This work sits inside a broader, active research push on **dermatology AI fairness**, motivated largely by the influential 2022 *Science Advances* paper "Disparities in dermatology AI performance on a diverse, curated clinical image set" (Daneshjou et al.) — the paper that introduced the DDI dataset this study also uses, and showed existing dermatology AI models drop 29-40% in accuracy on it.
- **Prior/related work from the same Rutgers group**: SAFE (Almuzaini, Dendukuri, Singh, 2023) — the algorithm-centric fairness framework FAIR-SCAN builds on top of — and a newer 2026 preprint, **SkinGuardian** (on-device, private, fair, explainable skin cancer detection), suggesting this is an active, ongoing research thread rather than a one-off paper.
- Broader academic landscape: multiple 2024-2026 papers tackle the same bias problem from different angles — **FairDisCo** (disentanglement contrastive learning), **FEDD** (diffusion-based fair lesion segmentation), and skin-tone normalization/channel-pruning approaches — most are algorithm-centric, which is exactly the gap FAIR-SCAN is positioned against (data-centric instead).
- On the clinical-deployment side, the general finding that dermatology AI datasets skew toward light skin (often under 5% representation of Fitzpatrick V-VI) is well-documented across systematic reviews as of 2026 — this is a known structural gap in the field data, not a one-paper problem.
- No commercial product is being sold here — this is academic, university-affiliated research (JAMIA is the fields main health-informatics journal), not a startup or deployed clinical tool.

## Open Problems & Bottlenecks

- **Data scarcity for darker skin tones remains the fundamental bottleneck** — the papers own limitation section notes there simply are not many large, pathology-confirmed dermatology datasets with strong dark-skin representation to work with; FAIR-SCAN optimizes what exists rather than solving the underlying scarcity.
- **Binary benign/malignant labeling loses clinically important nuance** — a slow-growing basal cell carcinoma and an urgent nodular melanoma are treated identically in this frameworks current labels, even though they demand very different urgency. The authors flag continuous risk-scoring or multi-class staging as the natural next step.
- **Computational cost of the ranking step itself** — even with their simplified (non-exhaustive) approximation of Shapley values, scoring every candidate image against a baseline model one at a time is expensive; true Shapley-value computation is described as highly computationally inefficient, which is why they built a cheaper approximation in the first place.
- **Real-world image quality was not tested** — they filtered out low-brightness/low-contrast images before training, which keeps the experiment clean but means FAIR-SCAN has not been validated on the messier, unfiltered photos a real telehealth app or phone camera would produce.
